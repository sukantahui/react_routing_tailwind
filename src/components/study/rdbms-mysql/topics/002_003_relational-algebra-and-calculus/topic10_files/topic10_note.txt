1. THE DIVISION OPERATOR (÷) DEFINITION & MATHEMATICAL SEMANTICS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The Division operator (denoted by the division symbol, $\div$) is a specialized binary
relational algebra operator designed to answer queries containing universal quantification ("FOR ALL").

MATHEMATICAL PROBLEM STATEMENT:
Given:
- Relation $R(A_1, \dots, A_n, B_1, \dots, B_m)$ of degree $n + m$
- Relation $S(B_1, \dots, B_m)$ of degree $m$ (where attributes of $S$ are a proper subset of $R$).

The Division $R \div S$ returns all tuples $t_A \in \pi_A(R)$ such that FOR EVERY tuple $t_S \in S$,
the combined tuple $t_A \circ t_S$ exists in relation $R$:
$$R \div S = \{ t_A \in \pi_A(R) \mid \forall t_S \in S, \ (t_A \circ t_S) \in R \}$$

KEY MATHEMATICAL PROPERTIES:
1. DEGREE (ARITY) REDUCTION:
   $$\text{Degree}(R \div S) = \text{Degree}(R) - \text{Degree}(S) = (n + m) - m = n$$
2. CARDINALITY BOUNDS:
   $$0 \le |R \div S| \le |\pi_A(R)| \le |R|$$
3. INVERSE OF CARTESIAN PRODUCT:
   If $T = R \times S$, then $T \div S = R$.


2. FORMAL 4-STEP DERIVATION USING PRIMITIVE OPERATORS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Since commercial SQL engines do not have a native `DIVIDE BY` keyword, Division is derived
using standard Projection, Cartesian Product, and Set Difference:

• STEP 1: All Candidate Values of $A$:
  $$T_1 = \pi_A(R)$$
• STEP 2: All Possible Combinations of Candidates with $S$:
  $$T_2 = T_1 \times S = \pi_A(R) \times S$$
• STEP 3: Combinations that DID NOT OCCUR (Disqualifiers):
  $$T_3 = T_2 - R = (\pi_A(R) \times S) - R$$
• STEP 4: Qualified Candidates (Universal Winners):
  $$R \div S = T_1 - \pi_A(T_3) = \pi_A(R) - \pi_A((\pi_A(R) \times S) - R)$$


3. SQL IMPLEMENTATIONS (THE 2 PRODUCTION PATTERNS)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PATTERN 1: DOUBLE `NOT EXISTS` (Direct Translation of Universal Quantifier $\forall x \equiv \neg \exists \neg$)
-- "Find students who have enrolled in ALL required core courses"
SELECT s.student_id, s.full_name
FROM students s
WHERE NOT EXISTS (
    SELECT c.course_id FROM core_courses c
    WHERE NOT EXISTS (
        SELECT 1 FROM enrollments e
        WHERE e.student_id = s.student_id AND e.course_id = c.course_id
    )
);

PATTERN 2: `GROUP BY` & `HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT)` (High-Performance Industrial Standard)
SELECT e.student_id, s.full_name
FROM enrollments e
JOIN students s ON e.student_id = s.student_id
WHERE e.course_id IN (SELECT course_id FROM core_courses)
GROUP BY e.student_id, s.full_name
HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT e.course_id) = (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM core_courses);

5. COMMON PITFALLS & DANGER ZONES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Forgetting `DISTINCT` inside `COUNT(e.course_id)`:
   - If a student retakes Course 101 twice, `COUNT(*)` equals 2 even if they only took 1 unique course!
2. Non-Matching Attribute Subsets:
   - Relation $S$ attributes must be a strict subset of $R$ with compatible domains.
