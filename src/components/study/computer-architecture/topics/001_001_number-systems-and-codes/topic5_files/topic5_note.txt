1. EXECUTIVE OVERVIEW & ARCHITECTURAL DEFINITION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Sign-Magnitude is the most intuitive and direct method for encoding signed integers
in binary hardware. It directly mimics human arithmetic notation: a number consists
of a distinct algebraic sign (+ or -) followed by its absolute magnitude.

In an n-bit Sign-Magnitude register:
- Bit (n-1) [MSB]: Sign Bit (0 = Positive '+', 1 = Negative '-')
- Bits (n-2) down to 0: Unsigned Binary Magnitude (absolute value |V|)

Formula:
  V = (-1)^(b_{n-1}) * Sum_{i=0}^{n-2} (b_i * 2^i)

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. NUMERICAL RANGE & THE DUAL ZERO ANOMALY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
For an n-bit Sign-Magnitude system:
- Positive Maximum: 0111...1111 = +(2^(n-1) - 1)
- Negative Minimum: 1111...1111 = -(2^(n-1) - 1)
- Total Range: [-(2^(n-1) - 1) to +(2^(n-1) - 1)]
- Total distinct numerical values = 2^n - 1

Range Examples:
  4-bit:  [-7 to +7]     (8-bit: [-127 to +127])
  16-bit: [-32,767 to +32,767]
  32-bit: [-2,147,483,647 to +2,147,483,647]

THE DUAL ZERO ANOMALY:
Sign-Magnitude suffers from two distinct binary representations of ZERO:
  +0 = 0000 0000 (Sign = 0, Mag = 0)
  -0 = 1000 0000 (Sign = 1, Mag = 0)

Hardware Consequences of Dual Zero:
1. Wastes one binary pattern out of the 2^n available states.
2. Complicates zero-checking circuits (testing `if (x == 0)` requires an OR gate
   combining +0 and -0 conditions rather than a single simple zero detector).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. STEP-BY-STEP WORKED EXAMPLES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMPLE 1: Represent +43 and -43 in 8-Bit Sign-Magnitude
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Step 1: Convert magnitude 43 to 7-bit binary:
  43 = 32 + 8 + 2 + 1 = (0101011)_2
Step 2: Attach Sign Bit (0 for positive, 1 for negative):
  +43 = 0 0101011  (Hex: 0x2B)
  -43 = 1 0101011  (Hex: 0xAB)

EXAMPLE 2: Decode 8-Bit Sign-Magnitude Pattern `10011100`
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Step 1: Inspect MSB (bit 7) = 1 → Sign is NEGATIVE (-).
Step 2: Inspect remaining 7 magnitude bits: 0011100.
Step 3: Calculate magnitude: 16 + 8 + 4 = 28.
Step 4: Combine sign and magnitude → Result = -28.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. WHY SIGN-MAGNITUDE WAS REPLACED IN INTEGER ALUs
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
While Sign-Magnitude is intuitive to humans, it is extremely inefficient for
hardware addition and subtraction circuits:
1. To add A + B, the ALU must first compare their signs:
   - If signs are the same, it must perform Addition and keep the sign.
   - If signs are different, it must use a Magnitude Comparator to determine whether
     |A| > |B| or |B| > |A|, perform Subtraction (|larger| - |smaller|), and assign
     the sign of the larger operand.
2. This requires separate adder circuits, subtractor circuits, multiplexers, and
   complex control logic, slowing down processor clock frequencies.

MODERN SURVIVAL: Where is Sign-Magnitude still used?
- IEEE-754 Floating-Point Standard: All single and double precision floats use
  a 1-bit sign field separated from the biased exponent and fractional mantissa.
- Audio DSP DACs & Bipolar ADCs with dedicated sign polarity pins.
