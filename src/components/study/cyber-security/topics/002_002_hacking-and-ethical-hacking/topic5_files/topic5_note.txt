1. EXECUTIVE OVERVIEW & DEFINITION OF GREY HAT HACKERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Grey Hat Hackers occupy the complex, legally precarious middle ground of the cybersecurity 
spectrum. 

They probe, scan, and penetrate computer systems WITHOUT PRIOR WRITTEN AUTHORIZATION 
(violating the law like Black Hats), but WITHOUT MALICIOUS INTENT to steal money or destroy 
data (aiming to disclose flaws, earn bug bounties, or help vendors patch like White Hats).

KEY LEGAL REALITY:
  Under Indian cyber jurisprudence (Section 43 & Section 66 of IT Act 2000) and international 
  law (US CFAA), unauthorized access is a strict liability offense. Subjective "good intent" 
  offers ZERO legal immunity against criminal prosecution.

3. ICONIC HISTORICAL GREY HAT CASE STUDIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

[CASE 1] KHALIL SHREATEH & MARK ZUCKERBERG'S FACEBOOK WALL (2013)
  * Action: Discovered a bug allowing anyone to post links onto any user's private wall.
  * Escalation: After Facebook triage engineers repeatedly dismissed his bug bounty report, 
    he demonstrated the exploit by posting directly onto Mark Zuckerberg's personal wall.
  * Outcome: Facebook patched the flaw in 2 hours, but withheld the bounty payout because 
    his demonstration violated policy by attacking an unauthorized live user account.

[CASE 2] LINUX.WIFATCH (2014 - 2015)
  * Action: A peer-to-peer worm that infected vulnerable IoT routers via default Telnet passwords.
  * Altruistic Behavior: Rather than installing botnets or cryptominers, Wifatch terminated 
    malicious malware processes, closed dangerous Telnet ports, and displayed a warning urging 
    owners to change their passwords.
  * Legal Status: Spreading an unauthorized self-propagating worm remains illegal worldwide.

[CASE 3] BRICKERBOT (2017 - "Janit0r")
  * Action: Scanned for default-credential IoT devices and executed Permanent Denial of 
    Service (PDoS) by overwriting flash memory (`/dev/mtd`) with zeros, bricking the hardware.
  * Rationale: The author claimed destroying insecure cameras prevented them from joining 
    Mirai DDoS botnets, forcing manufacturers to build better security.

[CASE 4] ADRIAN LAMO ("The Homeless Hacker" - Early 2000s)
  * Action: Compromised The New York Times, Microsoft, and LexisNexis from public cybercafes, 
    added his name to confidential expert directories, and then called CISOs to help them patch.
  * Outcome: Convicted under the US CFAA and sentenced to probation and restitution.

4. VULNERABILITY DISCLOSURE PARADIGMS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure (CVD - Industry Standard):
   - Practiced by Google Project Zero, CERT-In, and ISO/IEC 29147.
   - Researcher discovers a bug -> Privately informs vendor security team.
   - Standard 90-Day Grace Period: Vendor is granted 90 days to develop and release a patch.
   - Day 90: Coordinated joint release of the vulnerability advisory to the public.

2. Full Disclosure (Immediate Public 0-Day Drop):
   - Publishing exploit code directly onto GitHub or social media with zero vendor notice.
   - Pro: Forces vendor to act immediately without downplaying severity.
   - Con: Leaves millions of innocent end users vulnerable before a security patch exists.

3. Vulnerability Extortion (Criminal Boundary):
   - Demanding financial payment under threats of selling the flaw or leaking data publicly.
   - Punishable under Indian Penal Code (IPC 384 Extortion) and IT Act Section 66.

5. RFC 9116 AND MODERN SAFE HARBOR PROTOCOLS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. `security.txt` Standard (RFC 9116):
   - A standardized machine-readable file hosted at `https://example.com/.well-known/security.txt`.
   - Contains:
     * `Contact: mailto:security@example.com`
     * `Encryption: https://example.com/pgp-key.asc`
     * `Policy: https://example.com/responsible-disclosure`

2. Bug Bounty Safe Harbor Commitments:
   - Legally binding pledges by organizations (e.g., HackerOne Gold Standard Safe Harbor) 
     promising not to initiate civil or criminal legal action against ethical researchers 
     who comply with disclosure policy rules.

7. PROFESSIONAL TIPS & COMMON PITFALLS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

* PROFESSIONAL DEFENSIVE HABITS:
  1. Publish RFC 9116 `security.txt`: Make it easy for ethical researchers to contact you.
  2. Implement Legal Safe Harbor: Protect good-faith researchers from aggressive lawsuits.
  3. Adhere to 90-Day CVD Timelines: Give developers reasonable time to test complex patches.

* COMMON BEGINNER MISTAKES:
  - Scanning live websites without checking for a formal Vulnerability Disclosure Policy.
  - Dumping live database records to 'prove' an SQL injection vulnerability exists.
  - Demanding money or bounties before providing vulnerability details (Extortion).
  - Publicly leaking exploits on social media when triage responses take longer than expected.
