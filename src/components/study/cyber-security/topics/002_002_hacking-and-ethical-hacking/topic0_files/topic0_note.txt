1. EXECUTIVE OVERVIEW & HISTORICAL ROOTS OF HACKING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The word 'Hacking' originally emerged in the late 1950s and 1960s at the Massachusetts 
Institute of Technology (MIT) Tech Model Railroad Club (TMRC). Early computing 
pioneers used the term 'hack' to describe an elegant, ingenious, and unconventional 
solution to a complex engineering challenge.

Over the decades, hacking evolved through distinct historical eras:
  * 1970s (Phone Phreaking Era): John Draper ('Captain Crunch') and early telephone 
    explorers discovered that a 2600 Hz audio tone whistled into a telephone receiver 
    tricked AT&T trunk switches into resetting routing, enabling free calls.
  * 1980s-1990s (The Personal Computer & BBS Era): As dial-up modems spread, hacking 
    expanded into exploration of corporate mainframes, Bulletin Board Systems (BBS), 
    and software protection cracking.
  * 2000s-Present (Cyber Warfare & Enterprise Defense): The commercialization of the 
    internet transformed hacking into a multi-billion-rupee industry spanning state-sponsored 
    espionage, cybercrime syndicates, and professional enterprise ethical hacking.

2. CORE CYBERSECURITY TERMINOLOGY & DEFINITIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

+----------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| TERM                 | TECHNICAL DEFINITION & PRACTICAL CONTEXT                                |
+----------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. Vulnerability     | A flaw, bug, or design weakness in software/hardware/process that can   |
|                      | be leveraged to violate security policies (e.g., unescaped SQL query).   |
+----------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 2. Threat            | Any circumstance, actor, or event with the potential to adversely      |
|                      | impact an asset through unauthorized access, destruction, or denial.    |
+----------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 3. Exploit           | A crafted sequence of commands, script, or binary engineered to trigger |
|                      | a specific vulnerability and gain unauthorized system behavior.         |
+----------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 4. Payload           | The actual action or code executed on the target machine upon successful|
|                      | exploitation (e.g., spawning a reverse shell `/bin/sh` or encrypter).   |
+----------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 5. Zero-Day (0-Day)  | A vulnerability unknown to the software vendor with 0 days of official  |
|                      | patches available, leaving systems exposed to active exploitation.      |
+----------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 6. Attack Surface    | The total sum of all exposed network ports, APIs, web apps, and human   |
|                      | touchpoints accessible by an unauthorized adversary.                    |
+----------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 7. Risk              | The likelihood of a threat exploiting a vulnerability multiplied by the |
|                      | resulting financial, operational, and legal impact: Risk = T x V x I.   |
+----------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

   [ PHASE 1: RECONNAISSANCE ]
        |  → Passive OSINT, DNS records, Subdomain discovery (crt.sh)
        v
   [ PHASE 2: SCANNING & ENUMERATION ]
        |  → Port scanning (Nmap), Service version probing, Banner grabbing

4. THREAT ACTOR SPECTRUM & MOTIVATIONAL TAXONOMY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Script Kiddies (Amateurs):
   - Motivation: Personal ego, vandalism, trolling, social media bragging.
   - Skill: Low. Reliant entirely on pre-built GUI tools (LOIC, Metasploit, SQLmap).
   - Stealth: Zero. Easily detected by standard firewall signatures.

2. Hacktivists (Politically Motivated):
   - Motivation: Ideological, political, or social causes (e.g., Anonymous).
   - Tactics: Website defacements, DDoS takedowns, leaking embarrassing emails.

3. Cyber Criminal Syndicates (Financial Motive):
   - Motivation: Direct monetary extortion, ransomware, stolen credit cards.
   - Tactics: Double-extortion ransomware (DarkSide, LockBit), banking trojans.

4. Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs - Nation-State):
   - Motivation: Geopolitical espionage, intellectual property theft, military sabotage.
   - Resources: Multi-million-rupee state budgets, custom zero-day exploits, elite kernel rootkits.
   - Stealth: Extreme. Dwell time often spans 6 to 18 months undetected.

5. THREAT MODELING & SCORING FRAMEWORKS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. STRIDE THREAT MODELING (Microsoft):
   - S : Spoofing Identity        → Defense: Strong Authentication (FIDO2, TLS)
   - T : Tampering with Data      → Defense: Cryptographic HMACs & Hashes
   - R : Repudiation              → Defense: Immutable WORM Audit Logs
   - I : Information Disclosure   → Defense: AES-256 Encryption & Egress Lockdown
   - D : Denial of Service        → Defense: Anycast DDoS Scrubbing & Rate Limiting
   - E : Elevation of Privilege   → Defense: Principle of Least Privilege (RBAC)

2. COMMON VULNERABILITY SCORING SYSTEM (CVSS v3.1):
   - Numerical score ranging from 0.0 to 10.0 based on Base Metrics:
     * Attack Vector (Network, Adjacent, Local, Physical)
     * Attack Complexity (Low, High)
     * Privileges Required (None, Low, High)
     * User Interaction (None, Required)
     * Scope (Unchanged, Changed)
     * C-I-A Impact (None, Low, High)
   - Severity Bands:
     * Low: 0.1 - 3.9  |  Medium: 4.0 - 6.9  |  High: 7.0 - 8.9  |  Critical: 9.0 - 10.0

6. LEGAL FRAMEWORKS & INDIAN CYBER LAW
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Information Technology (IT) Act, 2000:
   - Section 43: Civil liability for unauthorized computer access, data downloading, 
     or introducing viruses without owner consent (Compensation to victims).
   - Section 66: Criminal penalty for hacking with fraudulent or dishonest intent 
     (Imprisonment up to 3 years, fine up to ₹5,00,000, or both).
   - Section 70B: Establishes CERT-In as the national nodal agency for incident handling.

2. CERT-In Mandatory Cybersecurity Directives:
   - All corporate and government organizations in India must report specified 
     cybersecurity incidents to CERT-In within 6 hours of discovery.

3. Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023:
   - Penalties up to ₹250 Crores for entities failing to implement reasonable security 
     safeguards resulting in personal data breaches.

8. PROFESSIONAL TIPS & COMMON PITFALLS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

* PROFESSIONAL HABITS:
  1. Always Secure Written Authorization: Never run active scans against an IP or 
     domain without signed Rules of Engagement (RoE) from the legal owner.
  2. Master Fundamentals Over Tools: Understand TCP/IP handshakes, assembly, and HTTP 
     protocols before running automated exploitation scripts.
  3. Practice Responsible Disclosure: Give software vendors 90 days to release 
     patches before publishing technical vulnerability analyses.

* COMMON BEGINNER MISTAKES:
  - Scanning public IP addresses from home broadband without legal permission (Section 66 violation).
  - Confusing a Vulnerability (the flaw) with an Exploit (the trigger) or Payload (the action).
  - Blindly executing downloaded GitHub exploit scripts that contain hidden backdoors.
  - Relying exclusively on automated scanners while missing deep business logic flaws.
